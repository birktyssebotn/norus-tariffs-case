Norway’s Exports to the U.S., and What the New Tariff Puts at Stake

A short applied data-analysis case study

Author

Birk Tyssebotn

Published

September 9, 2026

Background

In late July 2026, the United States imposed an additional 12.5% tariff on Norwegian goods, replacing the temporary 10% global tariff that expired on 24 July 2026. The U.S. justified the higher rate under a Section 301 investigation into forced labour in global supply chains. The Norwegian government has publicly rejected that justification.1

The OECD’s 2026 Economic Survey of Norway characterizes the country as relatively insulated from U.S. trade policy, since the U.S. takes well under 4% of Norway’s total exports.2 The numbers below sit inside that picture. While aggregate exposure is low, some industries will, naturally, have greater exposure than others.

The tariff is barely a month old at the time of writing, and SSB’s trade statistics lag by about a month. There is not yet any meaningful post-tariff data to analyse. Instead of forcing a before/after analysis with lacking data, this case study aims at answering the following questions:

  1. How much, and from which industries, does Norway export to the U.S., and how dependent is each industry on the U.S. market?
  2. How stable is that trade normally, seasonally and otherwise, before any policy shock, so a future change can be judged against a real baseline?
  3. How much is actually at stake: can this new tariff account for meaningful change in exports?

Data

Source: Statistics Norway (SSB), StatBank Table 08806, External trade in goods, by commodity group, country, month and imports/exports. Data are licensed under CC BY 4.0.

Industries are classified using the UN’s Standard International Trade Classification (SITC) at the 1-digit level. This is broad enough to read at a glance and granular enough to show which sectors are exposed to the tariff.

The data consists of three SSB extracts, with same commodity/exports settings, but differing in country and time span, plus one exchange rate series pulled live:

A seasonally-adjusted baseline

Exports naturally rise and fall by season and vary month to month for reasons that have nothing to do with policy. To judge whether a given month is actually unusual, that ordinary variation has to be removed first, leaving only the part that cannot be explained by any “normal” pattern.

Using nine years of history (2018 to present; fewer years would not support a reliable seasonal estimate), each industry’s monthly exports are decomposed via STL (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess) on the log scale into trend, seasonal, and remainder components. The remainder’s historical standard deviation defines “normal”: a month is flagged as out of the normal range if its remainder exceeds 2 standard deviations, larger than seasonality and typical random variation alone would usually produce. “9 Other commodities and transactions” is excluded. It is too small and irregular for a meaningful seasonal pattern.

Keep in mind that crossing two standard deviations is not a claim of real-world significance, only that a month’s move is statistically larger than that industry’s typical noise. It is used here to calibrate expectations: even at the April 2025 tariff shock, only a handful of industries experienced “unusual exports”, and only “Food and live animals” in the negative direction. Most shocks do not flag many, let alone every, industry at once.

As stated in the introduction, there is sparse “post-tariff” data available at the time of writing (July is latest as of early September). The following table is basically a binary check of whether the latest month of data is out of normal range or not. As of today, all industries exhibit “normal” exports, but this can be used for future reference as new data is published.

Code 
us_seasonal %>%
  filter(date == max(date)) %>%
  transmute(
    Industry = industry,
    `Actual (NOK mill.)` = round(exports_mill_nok, 0),
    `Seasonally expected (NOK mill.)` = round(expected_mill_nok, 0),
    `Out of normal range?` = if_else(out_of_normal_range, "Yes", "No")
  ) %>%
  arrange(desc(`Out of normal range?`)) %>%

Controlling for the exchange rate

The tariff is not the only thing changing the price of Norwegian goods for US buyers. If the krone weakens against the dollar, Norwegian exports get cheaper in USD terms, which can offset some of the tariff’s effect. If the krone strengthens, the opposite happens, and the tariff compounds on top of an already-rising USD price.

Code 
fx_start <- fx %>% filter(date == as.Date("2025-01-01")) %>% pull(usd_nok)
fx_tariff_month <- fx %>% filter(date == as.Date("2026-07-01")) %>% pull(usd_nok)
fx_latest <- fx %>% filter(date == max(date)) %>% pull(usd_nok)
fx_change_pct <- (fx_latest - fx_start) / fx_start * 100
fx_post_tariff_pct <- (fx_latest - fx_tariff_month) / fx_tariff_month * 100

ggplot(fx, aes(x = date, y = usd_nok)) +
  geom_line(color = "cadetblue", linewidth = 0.5) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = as.Date(c("2025-04-02", "2026-07-24")),
             linetype = "dashed", color = "grey40", linewidth = 0.3) +
  labs(
    x = NULL, y = "NOK per USD",
    title = "NOK/USD exchange rate",
    subtitle = "Dashed lines: Apr 2025 and Jul 2026 tariff dates.",
    caption = "Source: Norges Bank, series EXR/M.USD.NOK.SP."
  ) +
  theme_minimal() +
  theme(plot.title.position = "plot")

From January 2025 to August 2026 (the most recent month Norges Bank had published as of when this was last run), the rate moved from 11.34 to 9.43 NOK per USD, a change of -16.9%. A falling NOK-per-USD rate means the dollar buys fewer kroner, which means the krone strengthened over this window. That works against Norwegian exporters’ USD-denominated competitiveness, independent of the tariff.

From the tariff month itself (9.71 NOK per USD) to the latest available month, the change is −2.9%. That is not unusually large: several single-month moves earlier in 2026, unrelated to any tariff, were similar in size or bigger.

Takeaways

Although machinery and transport equipment, and food, account for the largest share of Norway’s exports to the US, that reflects the overall size of those industries, not their exposure to US market. Animal and vegetable oils, fats and waxes is small in absolute value, but sends about a quarter of its total exports to the US, the highest share of any industry, and the least able to redirect sales elsewhere if US demand drops.

The April 2025 tariff shock pushed only a few industries outside their normal range, and only Food and live animals moved down. The current increase, from 10% to 12.5%, is smaller than that shock. Over the same period, the krone moved by more than that 2.5 percentage-point difference, for reasons unrelated to the tariff.

If US-bound exports fall in the months ahead, the exchange rate, order timing, and global demand could be just as much to blame as the tariff.

Notes on methodology

  • Units: NOK million, current prices (not inflation-adjusted).
  • Industry classification: SITC 1-digit, broad by design, for readability. A finer breakdown (2- or 3-digit SITC) would show whether, for example, farmed salmon specifically drives the seafood category.
  • “9 Other commodities and transactions” is excluded from the seasonal analysis. Several months are recorded as zero or suppressed in the source data, and the category is under 0.03% of total exports. It is too sparse and irregular for a seasonal pattern to be meaningful.
  • Exempt Industries: The new tariff exempts specific goods, including fuel, fertilizer, some food, and products already covered by separate tariffs (autos, metals, pharmaceuticals). These exemptions are defined at a finer product level than SITC 1-digit, so a category’s true exposure may be lower than its total export value suggests, mineral fuels (SITC 3) in particular is exempt outright.
  • Seasonal baseline method: STL decomposition on log-transformed monthly exports, s.window = "periodic" (assumes a stable seasonal pattern across the 2018-present window rather than one that drifts over time). The 2018-2021 portion includes the COVID trade disruption, which will inflate the estimated “normal” variation for some industries.
  • Exchange rate is pulled live, not from a static file. The 05_wrangle_exchange_rate.R script queries Norges Bank’s API directly at run time (series EXR/M.USD.NOK.SP), so August 2026 above reflects whatever was published as of the last time the pipeline ran, not a fixed download date. Re-running it later will pick up newer months automatically.
  • Reproducibility: re-running scripts/01_wrangle_exports.R, 02_wrangle_share_of_total.R, 03_wrangle_seasonal.R, and 05_wrangle_exchange_rate.R after downloading updated extracts regenerates every processed CSV and every figure in this document.

```{r}
    data = filter(us_seasonal, out_of_normal_range),
    aes(y = exports_mill_nok), color = "firebrick3", size = 1.2
  ) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = as.Date(c("2025-04-02", "2026-07-24")),
             linetype = "dashed", color = "grey40", linewidth = 0.3) +
  facet_wrap(~ industry, scales = "free_y", labeller = label_wrap_gen(28)) +
  labs(
    x = NULL, y = "Exports (NOK million)",
    title = "Actual exports vs. the seasonally-expected range",
    subtitle = "Grey band = normal range (+-2 SD of remainder). Red points = out of that range. Dashed lines: Apr 2025 and Jul 2026 tariff dates.",
    caption = "Source: Statistics Norway, Table 08806. Method: STL decomposition, log scale, 2018-present."
  ) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 8) +
  theme(plot.title.position = "plot")
```

Keep in mind that crossing two standard deviations is not a claim of real-world significance, only that a month's move is statistically larger than that industry's typical noise. It is used here to calibrate expectations: even at the April 2025 tariff shock, only a handful of industries experienced "unusual exports", and only "Food and live animals" in the negative direction. Most shocks do not flag many, let alone every, industry at once.

As stated in the introduction, there is sparse "post-tariff" data available at the time of writing (July is latest as of early September). The following table is basically a binary check of whether the latest month of data is out of normal range or not. As of today, all industries exhibit "normal" exports, but this can be used for future reference as new data is published.

## Controlling for the exchange rate

The tariff is not the only thing changing the price of Norwegian goods
for US buyers. If the krone weakens against the dollar, Norwegian exports
get cheaper in USD terms, which can offset some of the tariff's effect.
If the krone strengthens, the opposite happens, and the tariff compounds
on top of an already-rising USD price.

```{r}
#| label: fig-exchange-rate
#| fig-cap: "NOK per 1 USD, monthly average"

fx_start <- fx %>% filter(date == as.Date("2025-01-01")) %>% pull(usd_nok)
fx_tariff_month <- fx %>% filter(date == as.Date("2026-07-01")) %>% pull(usd_nok)
fx_latest <- fx %>% filter(date == max(date)) %>% pull(usd_nok)
fx_change_pct <- (fx_latest - fx_start) / fx_start * 100
fx_post_tariff_pct <- (fx_latest - fx_tariff_month) / fx_tariff_month * 100

ggplot(fx, aes(x = date, y = usd_nok)) +
  geom_line(color = "cadetblue", linewidth = 0.5) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = as.Date(c("2025-04-02", "2026-07-24")),
             linetype = "dashed", color = "grey40", linewidth = 0.3) +
  labs(
    x = NULL, y = "NOK per USD",
    title = "NOK/USD exchange rate",
    subtitle = "Dashed lines: Apr 2025 and Jul 2026 tariff dates.",
    caption = "Source: Norges Bank, series EXR/M.USD.NOK.SP."
  ) +
  theme_minimal() +
  theme(plot.title.position = "plot")
```

From `r format(as.Date("2025-01-01"), "%B %Y")` to
`r format(max(fx$date), "%B %Y")` (the most recent month Norges Bank had
published as of when this was last run), the rate moved from
`r round(fx_start, 2)` to `r round(fx_latest, 2)` NOK per USD, a change of
`r round(fx_change_pct, 1)`%. A falling NOK-per-USD rate means the dollar
buys fewer kroner, which means the krone strengthened over this window.
That works against Norwegian exporters' USD-denominated competitiveness,
independent of the tariff.

From the tariff month itself (9.71 NOK per USD) to the latest available month, the change is −2.9%. That is not unusually large: several single-month moves earlier in 2026, unrelated to any tariff, were similar in size or bigger.


## Takeaways

Although machinery and transport equipment, and food, account for the largest share of Norway's exports to the US, that reflects the overall size of those industries, not their exposure to US market. Animal and vegetable oils, fats and waxes is small in absolute value, but sends about a quarter of its total exports to the US, the highest share of any industry, and the least able to redirect sales elsewhere if US demand drops.

The April 2025 tariff shock pushed only a few industries outside their normal range, and only Food and live animals moved down. The current increase, from 10% to 12.5%, is smaller than that shock. Over the same period, the krone moved by more than that 2.5 percentage-point difference, for reasons unrelated to the tariff.

If US-bound exports fall in the months ahead, the exchange rate, order timing, and global demand could be just as much to blame as the tariff. 

## Notes on methodology

- **Units:** NOK million, current prices (not inflation-adjusted).
- **Industry classification:** SITC 1-digit, broad by design, for
  readability. A finer breakdown (2- or 3-digit SITC) would show whether,
  for example, farmed salmon specifically drives the seafood category.
- **"9 Other commodities and transactions"** is excluded from the
  seasonal analysis. Several months are recorded as zero or suppressed in
  the source data, and the category is under 0.03% of total exports. It is too sparse and irregular for a seasonal
  pattern to be meaningful.
- **Exempt Industries:** The new tariff exempts specific goods, including fuel, fertilizer, some food, and products already covered by separate tariffs (autos, metals, pharmaceuticals). These exemptions are defined at a finer product level than SITC 1-digit, so a category's true exposure may be lower than its total export value suggests, mineral fuels (SITC 3) in particular is exempt outright.
- **Seasonal baseline method:** STL decomposition on log-transformed
  monthly exports, `s.window = "periodic"` (assumes a stable seasonal
  pattern across the 2018-present window rather than one that drifts
  over time). The 2018-2021 portion includes the COVID trade disruption,
  which will inflate the estimated "normal" variation for some
  industries. 
- **Exchange rate is pulled live, not from a static file.** The
  `05_wrangle_exchange_rate.R` script queries Norges Bank's API directly
  at run time (series `EXR/M.USD.NOK.SP`), so `r format(max(fx$date), "%B %Y")`
  above reflects whatever was published as of the last time the pipeline
  ran, not a fixed download date. Re-running it later will pick up newer
  months automatically.
- **Reproducibility:** re-running `scripts/01_wrangle_exports.R`,
  `02_wrangle_share_of_total.R`, `03_wrangle_seasonal.R`, and
  `05_wrangle_exchange_rate.R` after downloading updated extracts
  regenerates every processed CSV and every figure in this document.
